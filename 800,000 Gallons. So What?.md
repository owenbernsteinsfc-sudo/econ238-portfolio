800,000 Gallons Per Day Sounds Horrible – But What Does This Really Mean?

When you read in a headline that some AI-powered data center would consume 800,000 gallons of water every single day, you will probably think that it is too much water.

But it is not necessarily wrong just because it is a huge number.

We discussed in our classes a lot that a huge number by itself is not informative. You need to know what kind of comparison you are using, for what kind of purpose the resource is going to be used, and what kinds of alternatives there are.

The University of Rochester, for example, is a very good one to use for illustration. The University and Medical Center consume about 250 million gallons of domestic water each year, which is approximately 685,000 gallons per day. Not to mention that it consumes gigantic amounts of electricity, natural gas, land and other resources.

If those numbers were all just laid out without any context, the University of Rochester would seem like something extremely detrimental to the environment. However, nobody is going to look at those numbers and tell us that the whole thing needs to be torn down and closed. We know the resources are being expended for useful reasons, to create education, medical services, scientific research, jobs, and more.

This same principle applies to the data center.

It does not mean that the data center is necessarily good, but it is not enough to simply note that it consumes many resources to decide that it should not exist.

For example, 800,000 gallons of water a day may be a very serious matter in an area where there is not enough water. At the same time, in an area with plenty of accessible water, such a figure will be far less relevant. It looks the same on paper but carries a very different real social cost.

Electricity is no different. While a data center may consume tremendous amounts of it, one of the things we need to consider is how much extra infrastructure costs in that case.

If the company pays for the new substation, transmission lines, or other investments, this case will be different compared to one when ordinary consumers will face higher bills due to the project.

And this point, I believe, is when the issues related to data centers become more significant and raise real economic concerns.

For instance, pollution can become one of them. If the data center has natural gas turbines, then the pollution generated by the data center will negatively affect nearby residents. It will mean that certain costs will be imposed on those who did not want to get involved in that. The same can apply to noise, traffic, use of groundwater, etc.

It is an economic problem since the costs imposed on those who do not want to be a part of that project will be higher than the costs that are imposed on the company building the data center.

The solution, however, should not include banning the project. The appropriate approach would be requiring the company to pay for all the costs it imposes.

Another aspect that is easy to overlook is the consequence of the city saying no.

If Rochester denies a data center, that does not automatically mean the data center will not get built. It can just move it to some other city.

It is possible that the second location has more negative environmental impact than Rochester in terms of dirty electricity supply, availability of water or strictness of environmental policies. In this case, the burden of paying for the environmental cost will fall on some other city but the overall negative impact will be just as bad or even worse.

The city does not need to agree to every project but it needs to look further than just the first consequence.

In my opinion, one reason why people take this issue so seriously is that there is no clear idea about the positive impact that the project makes. With a university or a hospital, the use of water and energy resources is immediately obvious. In the case of a massive data center, however, people just see an enormous building consuming water and electricity to feed AI and technology companies.

That will only make it appear wasteful, even if the numbers are comparable to other major establishments.

Ultimately, I don't believe that the relevant questions have anything to do with whether “this data center consumes massive quantities of water or electricity.”

But the real questions to ask are: How valuable are these resources? Who pays for them? Who reaps the rewards? Are any costs being imposed on others? And if we halt this construction right now, where else does it get built?

It is easy to understand why “uses 800,000 gallons of water per day” is an effective headline. But it's not enough information to determine whether the data center is good or bad.
