Building a New Environmental Economy
There is an effort by America to establish a new environmental economy, although sometimes it is challenging to establish the infrastructure required to achieve that. There are long delays in the process of getting approval, and there are arguments about the environmental and social effects. The main focus of our project is to examine the changes that will have to take place in order for America to develop such infrastructure.

For the United States to develop a proper environmental economy, the country needs to learn how to construct environmentally friendly power stations, power lines, and other infrastructure in such a way that does not make the process so cumbersome and unpredictable that the project becomes too costly. This issue can be understood when one takes into account Brian Potter’s elaboration of NEPA. This piece of legislation allows the government to review the environmental consequences while providing for participation of the people. However, the process may be very lengthy because of the review and lawsuit procedures that increase costs and reduce predictability of the process. One potential institutional reform might involve making the review process quicker and more reliable for projects that are designed with the goal of reducing emissions, like renewable energy and grid expansion projects. This would alter the incentives developers face, as there would be less uncertainty surrounding the time and costs of a particular project. It might lead to greater investment and implementation of projects. However, the benefit does not stop at the specific project but could spill over to other clean-energy projects by making the construction of more infrastructure easier and cheaper. The issue is that speeding up the process will potentially reduce important safeguards. An environmental assessment enables communities to highlight issues that may be overlooked by developers or governmental agencies. According to Potter, NEPA has led to governmental agencies considering other options that they otherwise wouldn’t have. The individuals who stand to gain from faster permits will be able to see the environmental benefits that come from climate policy but fail to recognize the individuals who live near the development and pay the price for the negative local environmental impacts. In addition, it ties into trust in institutions. When individuals already have a notion that agencies are trying to speed up development, telling them that the process needs to be sped up further may lead to mistrust. A point in our discussion that caused me to rethink my opinion about the issue concerned the possibility that the United States and China might have different approaches to infrastructure projects in terms of what considerations take precedence in each country. This comparison made me reflect on the extent to which our systems are built around the need to avoid mistakes versus how fast we can make the right choices. Although I still believe that environmental safeguards are important, I no longer take for granted that simply having more processes necessarily leads to better results in the environment.
