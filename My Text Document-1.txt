    <!-- Section 1: Opening Hook -->
    <section>
      <p>Storms haven't gotten less dangerous[cite: 1]. We've gotten better at not being where they hit — mostly[cite: 1]. Annual U.S. deaths from tornadoes, floods, and hurricanes have fallen for decades even as population and coastal development have expanded rapidly[cite: 1, 2]. But raw death counts hide the real story: population-adjusted rates fell even faster, which means the average American today faces a small fraction of the storm-death risk their grandparents did[cite: 1, 2].</p>
      <p>This isn't because storms weakened; it's because of building codes, early warning systems, insurance markets, and — more subtly — where and how we choose to live relative to where the danger is[cite: 1]. That last part, however, is getting complicated by internal migration into hazard-prone Sun Belt regions[cite: 1].</p>
    </section>

    <!-- Section 2: Storm Fatalities & The Denominator Effect -->
    <section>
      <h2>1. The Denominator Effect: Raw Counts vs. Mortality Rates</h2>
      <p>Between 1940 and 1979, the United States averaged 260 to 308 deaths per year from tornadoes, floods, and hurricanes combined[cite: 2]. By the 2010s and 2020s, that figure had dropped to 170–218 deaths annually[cite: 2]. While a ~30% drop in raw fatalities is impressive[cite: 2], it severely understates human adaptation[cite: 1, 2].</p>
      
      <!-- Chart 1: Raw vs Rate -->

      <p>During this same 85-year period, the U.S. population nearly tripled—growing from ~132 million in 1940 to over 333 million today[cite: 2]. When we divide fatalities by population to establish a true "death intensity" rate, per-capita mortality risk collapsed by approximately <strong>65%</strong> (falling from 1.85 deaths per million to ~0.65 per million)[cite: 2].</p>

    <!-- Section 3: Internal Migration -->
    <section>
      <h2>2. Exposure and Migration: Moving Into the Path of Hazard</h2>
      <p>U.S. Census data confirms a continuous southward and westward shift in the mean center of population[cite: 1]. Millions of Americans have relocated to Florida, Texas, Arizona, and the Carolinas[cite: 1]. However, a move to the Sun Belt is not automatically a move into a hurricane zone or floodplain[cite: 1]. Risk exposure depends entirely on micro-geography—whether development occurs on elevated inland ridges or immediate coastal wetlands[cite: 1].</p>
      <p>As the IPCC SREX report emphasizes, long-term growth in disaster losses is driven primarily by increased capital and asset exposure in hazard-prone areas rather than changes in storm frequency[cite: 1]. We are putting significantly more wealth and infrastructure directly in harm's way[cite: 1].</p>
    </section>

    <!-- Section 4: Heat vs Cold & Burke Paper -->
    <section>
      <h2>3. The Silent Killer: Extreme Heat vs. Epidemiological Cold</h2>
      <p>Unlike storms, NWS tracked heat fatalities show an alarming upward trend in recent years[cite: 2]. The catastrophic 1995 Chicago heat wave (1,021 deaths in a single year) highlighted that heat mortality is an exposure and access crisis—impacting elderly and low-income residents lacking reliable air conditioning or power grid access[cite: 1, 2].</p>

      <div class="callout-box">
        <div class="callout-title">Critical Clarity: NWS Counts vs. Burke et al. (NBER WP #34313)</div>
        <p>It is essential not to confuse two distinct numbers[cite: 1]. <strong>NWS fatality counts</strong> measure direct, immediate-cause deaths (e.g., direct heat stroke)[cite: 1]. Conversely, <strong>Burke et al.</strong> find that temperature accounts for <strong>5–12% of total global mortality</strong>[cite: 1]. This higher figure is an epidemiological attributable estimate—capturing broad health nudges where extreme temperatures exacerbate underlying cardiovascular or respiratory illness[cite: 1]. Crucially, Burke et al. prove that globally and across most regions, <strong>cold kills significantly more people than heat</strong>[cite: 1].</p>
      </div>
    </section>

    <!-- Section 5: Economic Analysis & Calculations -->
    <section>
      <h2>4. Economic Logic: Capital Relocation & Carbon Math</h2>
      <p>Economists evaluate risk as the product of Hazard and Exposure ($\text{Risk} = \text{Hazard} \times \text{Exposure}$)[cite: 1]. Managing exposure is frequently far more cost-effective than attempting to alter climate hazards directly[cite: 1].</p>

      <div class="math-card">
        <h4>William Nordhaus Coastal Capital Relocation Calculation</h4>
        <ul>
          <li>• <strong>Vulnerable Coastal Capital:</strong> ~$600 Billion in structures below 10 meters elevation in hurricane zones[cite: 1].</li>
          <li>• <strong>Lifespan / Depreciation:</strong> Assuming a 50-year structural lifespan ($\frac{1}{50}$ or 2% depreciating annually)[cite: 1].</li>
          <li>• <strong>Annual Relocation Cost:</strong> $\$600\text{B} \times 0.02 = \mathbf{\$12\text{ Billion / year}}$[cite: 1].</li>
          <li>• <strong>Economic Impact:</strong> At ~$28.5 Trillion US GDP, managed capital retreat costs just <strong>0.04% of annual GDP</strong>[cite: 1].</li>
        </ul>
      </div>

      <div class="math-card">
        <h4>Phone Charging Carbon Damage ("Diet Juice" Exercise)</h4>
        <ul>
          <li>• <strong>Annual Phone Electricity:</strong> ~5 kWh/year @ $0.15/kWh = <strong>$0.75 / year</strong>[cite: 1].</li>
          <li>• <strong>Carbon Damage (100% Coal):</strong> 4.25 kg CO2 emitted @ $185/ton Social Cost of Carbon = <strong>$0.79 / year</strong> in damages[cite: 1].</li>
          <li>• <strong>Takeaway:</strong> Individual micro-behaviors are dwarfed by structural power grid decarbonization and urban planning[cite: 1].</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
    </section>

    <!-- Section 6: Hold On / Skepticism -->

      <div class="callout-box" style="border-color: var(--accent-amber); background: rgba(196, 132, 29, 0.06);">
        <div class="callout-title" style="color: var(--accent-amber);">Hold On! Required Self-Skepticism</div>
        <p><strong>Caveat:</strong> We must avoid over-interpreting falling fatality rates as a pure policy triumph[cite: 1]. An 85-year window contains relatively few independent draws of catastrophic, high-category hurricanes hitting dense urban centers[cite: 1]. A single worst-case storm strike on Miami or New Orleans can alter decade averages instantly[cite: 1, 2]. Furthermore, NOAA reporting and cause-of-death coding standards have changed dramatically since 1940[cite: 1].</p>
      </div>
    </section>

      <p>Jackson Pollock’s 1933 painting, <em>Man With Hand Plow</em>, offers an enduring visual metaphor: human adaptation to environmental hazards is an active, incremental, and behavioral process[cite: 1]. We do not prevent storms from forming; we build stronger roofs, establish early warnings, manage coastal zones, and adapt through economic choices[cite: 1].</p>
