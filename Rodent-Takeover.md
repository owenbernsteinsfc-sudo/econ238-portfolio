<h1>🦫 The Rodent Water Authority</h1>
<p class="sub">Hire 300,000 beaver families to fix the Colorado River</p>
</header>

<div class="card" style="text-align:center">
<p style="margin:0;font-size:18px">The Colorado River is over-promised by about <b>1.5 million acre-feet every year</b>. We trapped out the West’s natural water storage for hats 150 years ago. <b>What if we put it back?</b></p>
</div>

<div class="grid">
<div class="card stat"><b>300,000</b><span>beaver complexes needed<br>(1.5M AF ÷ 5 AF each)</span></div>
<div class="card stat"><b>$900M</b><span>one-time build cost<br>(300,000 × $3,000 per dam analog)</span></div>
<div class="card stat"><b>~$30</b><span>per acre-foot per year<br>over 20 years</span></div>
</div>

<div class="card bars">
<h2>Cost per acre-foot of water</h2>
<div class="row"><div class="lab">Beaver dams</div><div class="track"><div class="fill" style="width:6%;background:var(--accent);min-width:64px">$30</div></div></div>
<div class="row"><div class="lab">Beavers, 5× worse</div><div class="track"><div class="fill" style="width:10%;background:var(--accent);min-width:70px">$150</div></div></div>
<div class="row"><div class="lab">Desalination</div><div class="track"><div class="fill" style="width:100%;background:var(--warm)">$1,000–$3,000</div></div></div>
<div class="note">Bars drawn roughly to scale. Beavers build the dams and maintain them for free.</div>
</div>

<div class="grid">
<div class="card">
<h2>Why it sounds crazy</h2>
<ul><li>A rodent as critical infrastructure</li><li>Western water law fears unpermitted storage</li><li>Engineers love concrete and gauges</li></ul>
</div>
<div class="card">
<h2>Why it’s less crazy</h2>
<ul><li><b>Externalities:</b> everyone downstream benefits for free</li><li><b>Property rights:</b> a tradable recharge credit pays ranchers who host beavers</li><li><b>Cheap:</b> 10 to 100× less than the usual fixes</li></ul>
</div>
</div>

<div class="card kill">
<h2>What could kill it</h2>
<p><b>Evaporation.</b> Open water in the arid West loses 3 to 6 feet a year. So aim for <b>groundwater recharge and slow runoff</b>: cool, high-elevation, shaded wetlands, not big sunny ponds. Methane is the other risk, so we monitor and shut down sites that don’t perform.</p>
<p><b>If we overdo it:</b> ponds must fill first, so downstream states and Mexico could see less water for a few years. Phase it in over ~10 years and slow down if flows drop.</p>
</div>

<div class="card">
<h2>Who wins, who loses, who pays</h2>
<div class="flow">
<div>Landowners<br><i>paid + liability cover</i></div><i>→</i>
<div>Beavers + BDAs<br><i>recharge &amp; slow flow</i></div><i>→</i>
<div>Recharge credits<br><i>sold to cities</i></div><i>→</i>
<div>Cheaper water<br><i>than new infrastructure</i></div>
</div>
<p class="note" style="text-align:center">Losers: engineering firms, senior-rights holders worried about predictable flows. Hardest step: reforming state storage law.</p>
</div>

<div class="card">
<h2>Why hasn’t anyone done this?</h2>
<p>Utah, Oregon, Washington and Colorado run pilots, but it hasn’t scaled. Incumbents want predictable flows, ranchers dislike flooded pasture, and nobody can send a bill for diffuse benefits. We underinvest in cheap, self-maintaining infrastructure because nobody profits from building it.</p>
</div>
