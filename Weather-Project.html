We Didn't Get Safer From Storms by Accident — We Got Safer by Moving

Why raw weather fatality counts hide an 80-year collapse in per-capita storm risk, and why exposure management matters more than hazard elimination.

By Owen Bernstein • Econ 238 Final Portfolio • Published September 2026 • Data: NOAA NWS (1940–2024), US Census Bureau, NBER WP #34313

Storms haven't gotten less dangerous. We've gotten better at not being where they hit — mostly. Annual U.S. deaths from tornadoes, floods, and hurricanes have fallen for decades even as population and coastal development have expanded rapidly. But raw death counts hide the real story: population-adjusted rates fell even faster, which means the average American today faces a small fraction of the storm-death risk their grandparents did.

This isn't because storms weakened; it's because of building codes, early warning systems, insurance markets, and — more subtly — where and how we choose to live relative to where the danger is.

1. The Denominator Effect: Raw Counts vs. Mortality Rates

Between 1940 and 1979, the United States averaged 260 to 308 deaths per year from tornadoes, floods, and hurricanes combined. By the 2010s and 2020s, that figure had dropped to 170–218 deaths annually. While a ~30% drop in raw fatalities is impressive, it severely understates human adaptation.

U.S. Extreme Weather Fatalities (1940s–2020s)
Decade annual averages for Tornado, Flood, and Hurricane combined. Note the 2000s Katrina outlier spike (1,016 deaths in 2005).

During this same 85-year period, the U.S. population nearly tripled—growing from ~132 million in 1940 to over 333 million today. When we divide fatalities by population to establish a true "death intensity" rate, per-capita mortality risk collapsed by approximately 65% (falling from 1.85 deaths per million to ~0.65 per million).

2. The Silent Killer: Extreme Heat vs. Epidemiological Cold

Unlike storms, NWS tracked heat fatalities show an alarming upward trend in recent years. The catastrophic 1995 Chicago heat wave (1,021 deaths in a single year) highlighted that heat mortality is an exposure and access crisis—impacting elderly and low-income residents lacking reliable air conditioning or power grid access.

Critical Distinction: NWS Counts vs. Burke et al. (NBER WP #34313)

It is essential not to confuse two distinct metrics. NWS fatality counts measure direct, immediate-cause deaths (e.g., direct heat stroke). Conversely, Burke et al. find that temperature accounts for 5–12% of total global mortality. This higher figure is an epidemiological attributable estimate—capturing broad health nudges where extreme temperatures exacerbate underlying cardiovascular or respiratory illness. Crucially, Burke et al. prove that globally and across most regions, cold kills significantly more people than heat.

3. Economic Logic: Capital Relocation & Carbon Math

Economists evaluate risk as the product of Hazard and Exposure (Risk = Hazard × Exposure). Managing exposure is frequently far more cost-effective than attempting to alter climate hazards directly.

William Nordhaus Coastal Capital Relocation Calculation

  • • Vulnerable Coastal Capital: ~$600 Billion in structures below 10 meters elevation in hurricane zones.
  • • Lifespan / Depreciation: Assuming a 50-year structural lifespan (1/50 or 2% depreciating annually).
  • • Annual Relocation Cost: $600B × 0.02 = $12 Billion / year.
  • • Economic Impact: At ~$28.5 Trillion US GDP, managed capital retreat costs just 0.04% of annual GDP.

Phone Charging Carbon Damage ("Diet Juice" Exercise)

  • • Annual Phone Electricity: ~5 kWh/year @ $0.15/kWh = $0.75 / year.
  • • Carbon Damage (100% Coal): 4.25 kg CO2 emitted @ $185/ton Social Cost of Carbon = $0.79 / year in damages.
  • • Takeaway: Individual micro-behaviors are dwarfed by structural power grid decarbonization and urban planning.
Hold On! Required Self-Skepticism

Caveat: We must avoid over-interpreting falling fatality rates as a pure policy triumph. An 85-year window contains relatively few independent draws of catastrophic, high-category hurricanes hitting dense urban centers. A single worst-case storm strike on Miami or New Orleans can alter decade averages instantly. Furthermore, NOAA reporting and cause-of-death coding standards have changed dramatically since 1940.

Conclusion & Ending Flourish

Jackson Pollock’s 1933 painting, Man With Hand Plow, offers an enduring visual metaphor: human adaptation to environmental hazards is an active, incremental, and behavioral process. We do not prevent storms from forming; we build stronger roofs, establish early warnings, manage coastal zones, and adapt through economic choices.
